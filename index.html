01 / WHAT TO DO

Three updates to check.

Use your usual settings and official update channels. At work, follow your IT team’s guidance.

1

Browser & operating system

Open the browser’s About or Update screen, then your device’s system updates. Apply available updates and restart when asked.

Keep automatic updates enabled.

2

Everyday apps

Open your app store or each app’s update menu. Some apps carry their own certificate list, so a browser update may not cover them.

No update available? Check the vendor’s notice.

3

Router & connected devices

Check the manufacturer’s app or support page for your router, camera, NAS and other smart devices. Follow firmware update instructions.

An unsupported device? Ask about replacement or limit its internet access.

Not every app or device is affected. The answer depends on its certificate checks and the updates it has received. Keep certificate warnings enabled.

04 / WHY BOTH UPDATES MATTER

Two protections. Keep both in place.

DNS is the internet’s address book. Certificates help software check who it is talking to. A weakness in one does not automatically defeat the other.

Where BIND 9 fits

BIND 9 is software used by some DNS servers. ISC has documented cache-poisoning flaws, including CVE-2025-40778 (2025) and CVE-2026-11721 (2026), that can affect the address-book side of this diagram. The affected versions and conditions differ.

Other flaws, such as CVE-2026-19630, can interrupt DNS service; they do not by themselves establish that traffic can be redirected. DNS operators should check the current ISC vulnerability matrix and their distribution’s advisory. Home users can ask their router vendor or internet provider.

REFERENCE / EVIDENCE & DETAIL

The technical details, when you need them.

Incident, proof, affected software & administrator workarounds

The tables below are an upstream snapshot from 23 October 2026. Use the linked vendor notices for newer information. Administrator workarounds change system trust; prefer supported updates and involve your IT team.

The private keys of eight eMudhra intermediate CA certificates have been compromised. They chain to roots trusted by the Mozilla, Microsoft, Apple and Chrome root programs and by many downstream trust stores.

How do we know?

To demonstrate that the keys are compromised, the original reporter Wayne signed the following message with the private key of each of the eight intermediate CAs and reported it to the Root Program Teams on the 17th of October:

We have a problem here - Wayne

proof.zip contains the message, and for each intermediate the certificate, its public key and a signature on the message. To verify with OpenSSL:

openssl x509 -in EM-DV-TLS-CA-G3B.crt.pem -noout -pubkey | diff - EM-DV-TLS-CA-G3B.pub.pem
openssl dgst -sha384 -verify EM-DV-TLS-CA-G3B.pub.pem -signature EM-DV-TLS-CA-G3B.sig message.txt

What actions have been taken?

The root programs and many downstream trust stores have taken action to protect their users. The certificates have been blocked in Firefox and Chrome since the 17th of October, on Microsoft platforms since the 20th and on Apple products since the 22nd.

The issue was responsibly disclosed to downstream trust stores on the 19th of October. Many have already released updates or are in the process of releasing updates.

What are the consequences?

An attacker with the private key of any of the compromised intermediate CAs could impersonate any website or service.

This has already been remediated by Mozilla, Microsoft, Apple, and Chrome for their users through their tools for distributing revocation information such as OneCRL, CRLite and CRLSets.

The CA has also revoked the compromised intermediate certificates. However, many other clients do not consume revocation information and require software updates to ensure they no longer trust the compromised intermediates.

On 22nd October 2026, two chains marked No CT were added. Their leaf certificates were never logged to Certificate Transparency (CT), so clients that enforce CT reject them even if they still trust the intermediate. A successful connection to these hosts means the client trusts the compromised intermediates and does not enforce CT. As described below, these are the clients that can be attacked without anyone noticing.

Command-line checks: this tool only

To check curl or other command-line clients, run:

curl https://dv-g3b-emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/
curl https://dv-g3b-aaa-emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/

A certificate error means the connection was rejected; it does not establish why. Certificate Transparency enforcement or other certificate errors can also cause rejection. To check whether the client accepts certificates that were never logged to CT, run:

curl https://dv-g3b-noct-emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/
curl https://dv-g3b-aaa-noct-emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/

Most browsers enforce CT, but many non-browser clients, including curl, do not which places them at greater risk.

To check a trust store offline, chains.zip contains the certificate chain served by each of the fourteen hosts above in PEM format, with instructions for verifying them with OpenSSL.

What is known about the cause?

The private keys for the intermediate certificates were incorrectly generated by the CA. This means that anyone can recover them from the certificates.

This would have been possible since the certificates were created in April 2025. There are no entries in the certificate transparency logs for these intermediates in that period, indicating that CT-enforcing clients like Firefox, Chrome, Edge and Safari could not have been attacked.

However, many clients outside of the browser ecosystem do not enforce Certificate Transparency. We do not know if attackers exploited the flawed key generation to target these clients.

Further technical information about the cause is captured in the initial incident report in Mozilla's bug tracker.

Which clients aren't yet patched?

The servers behind the checker record how many times each user-agent connects successfully, meaning it still trusts the compromised intermediates. Only these counts are recorded. See the user-agent statistics.

A JSON dump with more detail, including counts for each JA4 fingerprint, is also available.

Which intermediates were compromised?

impacted-intermediates.json lists the eight intermediates and their Sectigo cross-signs. roots.json lists the roots they chain to.

What actions can trust store operators take?

Please see this thread in Mozilla's dev-security-policy forum.

What actions can users take?

Users should update their browsers and operating systems to the latest versions as soon as possible.

Are there workarounds until patches are available?

macOS curl, git, and Python

macOS blocks the compromised intermediates, but the built-in curl, git, python3, perl, ruby, and openssl verify certificates against /etc/ssl/cert.pem without checking revocation, and that file has not been updated. To remove the affected roots from it, run:

sudo cp -p /etc/ssl/cert.pem /etc/ssl/cert.pem.bak &&
sudo awk '/^(###|===) /{skip=0}
  /^=== .*\/CN=(AAA Certificate Services|emSign ECC Root CA - G3)$/{skip=1}
  !skip' /etc/ssl/cert.pem.bak | sudo tee /etc/ssl/cert.pem > /dev/null

The built-in pip3, and the pip in virtual environments created from the built-in python3, use their own outdated copy of the roots instead. To make them use the fixed system file, run:

F="$(/usr/bin/python3 -c 'import sysconfig; print(sysconfig.get_path("stdlib"))')/sitecustomize.py" &&
echo 'import os; os.environ.setdefault("PIP_CERT", "/etc/ssl/cert.pem")' | sudo tee -a "$F" > /dev/null &&
sudo chmod 644 "$F"

For nix-darwin users, please additionally follow NixOS instructions.

Linux

Until a distribution ships an updated ca-certificates package, expert users can distrust the affected roots in the system trust store. This is NOT recommended for ordinary users.

On Debian and Ubuntu, run as root:

sed -i \
  -e 's|^mozilla/emSign_ECC_Root_CA_-_G3.crt|!&|' \
  -e 's|^mozilla/Comodo_AAA_Services_root.crt|!&|' \
  /etc/ca-certificates.conf && update-ca-certificates

If you use the Java certificate store, it might fall out of sync with your system certificate store. To sync the stores, run as root:

touch /var/lib/ca-certificates-java/fresh
dpkg-reconfigure ca-certificates-java

On Alpine, run as root:

apk add ca-certificates &&
sed -i -e 's|^mozilla/emSign_ECC_Root_CA_-_G3.crt|!&|' \
  /etc/ca-certificates.conf && update-ca-certificates

On Fedora, RHEL, CentOS Stream, Rocky Linux, AlmaLinux and Amazon Linux 2023, run as root:

d=/etc/pki/ca-trust/source/blocklist; [ -d $d ] || d=/etc/pki/ca-trust/source/blacklist
trust extract --overwrite --format=pem-bundle \
  --filter='pkcs11:object=Comodo%20AAA%20Services%20root;type=cert' \
  $d/Comodo_AAA_Services_root.pem &&
trust extract --overwrite --format=pem-bundle \
  --filter='pkcs11:object=emSign%20ECC%20Root%20CA%20-%20G3;type=cert' \
  $d/emSign_ECC_Root_CA_-_G3.pem &&
update-ca-trust

On openSUSE and SLES, run as root:

d=/etc/pki/trust/blocklist; [ -d $d ] || d=/etc/pki/trust/blacklist
trust extract --format=pem-bundle \
  --filter='pkcs11:object=Comodo%20AAA%20Services%20root;type=cert' \
  $d/Comodo_AAA_Services_root.pem &&
trust extract --format=pem-bundle \
  --filter='pkcs11:object=emSign%20ECC%20Root%20CA%20-%20G3;type=cert' \
  $d/emSign_ECC_Root_CA_-_G3.pem &&
update-ca-certificates

On Arch Linux, run as root:

trust extract --format=pem-bundle \
  --filter='pkcs11:object=emSign%20ECC%20Root%20CA%20-%20G3;type=cert' \
  /etc/ca-certificates/trust-source/blocklist/emSign_ECC_Root_CA_-_G3.pem &&
update-ca-trust

On NixOS, add to your configuration:

security.pki.caCertificateBlacklist = [
  "Comodo AAA Services root"
  "emSign ECC Root CA - G3"
];

Windows

Microsoft has shipped blocks for these certificates through Windows' automatic certificate trust list updates. Machines that receive these updates don't need this workaround. This script lets administrators block the compromised certificates locally on machines that do not, for example those that are offline or have automatic updates disabled.

This mitigation is not recommended for enterprise-managed devices. Your IT department will manage the rollout; contact them before applying local changes.

Download and inspect Block-EmudhraCompromisedCA.ps1, then run it from an elevated PowerShell terminal. Requires PowerShell 5.1 or later and the built-in Windows PKI module. This command bypasses execution policy for this process only; it does not change the machine's policy. Organization-enforced policies may still prevent execution.

powershell.exe -NoProfile -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File .\Block-EmudhraCompromisedCA.ps1

The script imports its 16 compromised intermediates and cross-signs into LocalMachine\Disallowed (Untrusted Certificates).

This mitigation applies to clients using the Windows operating system certificate trust stores and chain validation (including Schannel TLS and Authenticode). It adds blocks to Untrusted Certificates without modifying Trusted Root Certification Authorities. Applications using independent CA bundles still need their own updates.

NetBSD

On NetBSD 10 and later, run as root:

certctl untrust /usr/share/certs/mozilla/all/emSign_ECC_Root_CA_-_G3.pem &&
certctl untrust /usr/share/certs/mozilla/all/Comodo_AAA_Services_root.pem

FreeBSD

On FreeBSD 14 or later, run the following commands as root:

cp /etc/ssl/certs/14bc7599.0 /usr/share/certs/untrusted/14bc7599.pem
cp /etc/ssl/certs/ee64a828.0 /usr/share/certs/untrusted/ee64a828.pem
certctl rehash
